02 · Material

Read the
fibre.

No single home test proves fibre content. Compare several clues, preserve the saree, and use laboratory analysis when certainty matters.

CottonPolyester

Cotton

Soft, diffuse lustre

Short staple fibres can create a subtle halo and a less uniformly reflective surface.

Polyester

Smooth, even lustre

Continuous filaments often appear smoother and reflect light more uniformly.
Start with observation. Finishes, yarn construction and blends can change how either fabric looks.
You deserve to know what your saree is made of.

Polyester is a plastic-based synthetic fibre commonly produced from fossil resources. Synthetic textiles can release persistent microplastic fibres during production, wear, washing and disposal.

Fire behaviour matters too: polyester may melt and form hot material against skin, while cotton burns without melting. Neither should be described as fire-safe.

A buyer should not have to cut or ignite a saree to discover its composition. Ask the seller for clear fibre disclosure and supporting evidence; professional textile analysis is the reliable option when certainty matters.

03 · Construction

Woven, printed
or machine-made?

Read several construction clues together. One irregularity—or one perfectly repeated motif—cannot establish how an entire saree was made.

Turn it over

Woven motif

The motif structure usually participates in the cloth and can often be traced on the reverse.

Study the surface

Printed motif

Colour sits on the surface; the reverse may look paler and the yarn structure remains unchanged.

Follow the rhythm

Handloom clues

Small variations in beat, selvedge and motif placement may reflect hand-guided work—but are supporting clues, not proof.

Check repetition

Powerloom clues

Highly uniform repetition can suggest mechanised production, though skilled handloom work may also be exceptionally regular.

Best evidence Ask for fibre composition, producer or cooperative details, weave origin and any traceable certification—not only a visual claim.

04 · Provenance

What a GI
really tells you.

A Geographical Indication connects registered goods to a defined place where their quality, reputation or characteristics are essentially attributable to that origin.

01

Who registers it?

The Geographical Indications Registry, under Intellectual Property India, administers registration under India’s GI Act, 1999.

02

Who may use it?

A producer of registered goods may apply to be entered as an authorised user. The Registry maintains records of registered GIs and authorised users.

03

What does it prove?

GI status protects a registered geographical name and its specified product tradition. It does not make every seller’s use of that name authentic.

Verify before you buy

Follow the evidence,
not only the label.

  1. Match the nameCheck the exact registered GI—not a similar marketing phrase.
  2. Match the placeThe claimed production origin should agree with the registered geography.
  3. Ask the sourceRequest producer, cooperative, authorised-user or procurement information.
  4. Check the registerSearch the official GI records for the GI and authorised-user details.

c. 4000 BCE onward

Cloth before the modern sari

India’s handmade textile history reaches back at least six millennia. Ancient cotton knowledge preceded the garment we now call the saree, which emerged gradually from traditions of wrapped, unstitched cloth.

Try this: notice how one length becomes architecture on the body.
16th–17th centuries

Courts, workshops and fine muslin

Under Mughal patronage, regional workshops and established crafts reached wider markets. Bengal’s extraordinarily fine cottons and figured muslins became celebrated far beyond the subcontinent.

Look closer: Jamdani motifs are woven—not embroidered.
18th–19th centuries

Narratives enter the pallu

Baluchari developed as a celebrated Bengal silk tradition, its pallus arranged like woven picture galleries. Museum records preserve regional silk, brocade and cotton sarees from this era.

Decode it: a pallu can be read like a graphic novel.
19th–20th centuries

Industry, resistance and survival

Mechanised production transformed textile markets, yet handmade cloth endured. During the freedom movement, khadi and hand-spinning became expressions of self-reliance and political identity.

Ask of every saree: handloom or powerloom—and how do we know?
2013

Jamdani recognised by UNESCO

UNESCO inscribed Bangladesh’s traditional art of Jamdani weaving. Motifs are formed directly on the loom through a labour-intensive discontinuous-weft technique transmitted within weaving communities.

Human signature: tiny variations reveal the rhythm of the hand.
2025

Tangail joins the living-heritage list

Traditional saree weaving art of Tangail, Bangladesh, joined UNESCO’s Representative List—recognition of a tradition kept alive through practice and transmission.
